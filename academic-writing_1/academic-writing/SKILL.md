# Academic writing: journal papers (IB and marketing)

This skill is built from four sources. Two are Arne's own papers: a sole-drafted qualitative IB manuscript (startup internationalisation during disruptions, 3rd revision, 2026) and a co-authored meta-analysis (Blut, Teller & Floh, *Journal of Retailing*). The other two are exemplar meta-analyses in top marketing journals: Babic Rosario et al. (JMR 2016) and Barari, Eisend & Jain (JAMS 2026). The logic for building the introduction also draws on Jeromy Anglim's guide "How to Write the Introduction Section in Psychology" (2009). That guide is adapted here to IB and marketing conventions.

The goal is prose that reads like a careful, confident IB or marketing scholar wrote it. It should never read like a language model wrote it.

## 1. Voice in one paragraph

Write plain, precise, argument-driven academic English. Each paragraph makes one point, states it early, supports it with cited evidence, and ends by saying what follows from it. Use active voice with "we" (about 6-9 per 1,000 words). Use "our findings", "our analysis", and "this study" sparingly. Keep hedging calibrated rather than defensive. Claims are exactly as strong as the design allows. Use no ornament, no hype adjectives and no rhetorical questions. The only exception is a formally stated research question.

## 2. Measured style targets

- **Sentence length:** mean 26-28 words, median about 25, with real variation (SD about 13). Mix long, citation-bearing sentences with short declaratives that land the point. For example: "A weak professional tie is not always useful. Its value depends on whether founders can create, maintain, and strengthen it."
- **Voice:** mostly active. Use the passive only when the actor is irrelevant, e.g. "Interviews were recorded and transcribed".
- **Connectors:** prefer *however, thus, therefore, whereas, while, although, in addition, specifically, taken together, overall*. Use *moreover* and *furthermore* rarely. Never stack two connectors ("Specifically, we thus..."), and do not use *thus* twice in one paragraph.
- **Enumeration:** use "First, ... Second, ... Third, ..." for gaps, contributions, reasons, implications and limitations. Announce the count first: "We make two contributions." / "This is important for three reasons."
- **Punctuation:** do not use em dashes in running text. Use commas, parentheses, a colon or a new sentence instead. Use semicolons only between closely linked independent clauses and inside citation lists. Write "i.e.," and "e.g.," with a comma, and only inside parentheses.
- **Spelling:** default to American spelling (internationalization, behavior, analyze). Switch to British only if the target journal requires it, and then apply it everywhere. Never mix. Before finishing, check consistency for spelling, hyphenation (startup vs. start-up), capitalisation (COVID-19), and abbreviations (IB, B2B, WOM).

## 3. Section templates (the moves Arne uses)

### Abstract (150-250 words, one paragraph)
Follow these steps in order:

1. Phenomenon and tension in one sentence: "X constrains Y, yet some firms continue to..."
2. "We examine how..." or "We conduct a meta-analysis of N effect sizes across K studies..."
3. Data, with the concrete numbers.
4. Two to four findings, each concrete and ideally contrastive (before/during/after; stronger for A, whereas B...).
5. One closing sentence on what the findings show or imply.

Do not write "This paper aims to". Do not write "Implications are discussed."

### Introduction (about 4-6 paragraphs)

1. **Open with the field's attention, not a cliche:** "There has been growing interest in X and its effects on Y (cites)." Then state what this stream shows. Exemplar alternative: open with a concrete market fact, such as a spending figure or adoption numbers with a source.
2. **Motivate the problem with evidence.** Contrast findings or cases, e.g. "While some startups internationalized less during the pandemic (cite), others initiated or continued their expansion (cite)." End the paragraph with the puzzle: "This raises the question of why..."
3. **Locate the gap precisely.** Say what is established, then what is not: "IB research has long established X (cites). However, we know less about how..." / "What remains less understood is...". Prefer *underexplored*, *we know less*, or *remains unclear* over the word "gap". Where useful, reframe: "The unresolved question is therefore not whether X, but how Y."
4. **State the research question explicitly** when the design is exploratory: "Specifically, we address the following research question: How do...?"
5. **Briefly preview design and data**, with numbers.
6. **Contributions:** "We make two contributions. First, we show that... Second, we show that..." Each one names the literature it speaks to. Follow with one or two sentences on practical or policy implications.

### Introduction logic: Gap × Importance × Method

An introduction is justified only when all three factors are present (Anglim's justification formula). If any one of them is zero, the case for the paper collapses. Before drafting, write one sentence for each factor:

1. **Gap:** what is unknown, disputed or flawed.
2. **Importance:** why closing this gap matters.
3. **Method:** why this design can actually close it.

Name the **gap type**, because it determines the argument you have to make:

- Theory contradicts the empirical findings.
- Competing theories or schools of thought make different predictions.
- Existing studies share a methodological flaw, e.g. common-method bias, cross-sectional designs, student samples, single-country data, or endogeneity.
- The topic has not been studied at all.
- There is too little evidence, or the findings are inconsistent and have never been synthesised.

Verify the gap before you claim it:

- Evidence it from recent, influential articles, and show differing views where they exist.
- For a claim that no research exists, check database searches, recent reviews and forward citations first. Then use a bounded statement: "To the best of our knowledge, no study has examined...". If the search has not been done, tell the user rather than asserting the claim.

**Importance** has to be argued, not just asserted. Pick one or more of these approaches:

- Name the practical stakes: managers, policymakers, firms affected, money spent. Cite a sourced figure.
- Show scholarly attention: influential authors who call the question important, or bibliometric evidence of a growing literature.
- Show the scale of the problem with statistics.

Keep two things apart. One is why the *gap* matters. The other is why *this study's design* can close it. An important gap does not justify a weak design.

Map the **method** to the gap explicitly:

| Gap | Fitting design |
|---|---|
| No research on the topic | Exploratory or qualitative study, or first empirical test |
| Methodological flaws in prior work | A design that removes the flaw (e.g. longitudinal, multi-source, experimental, IV or panel) |
| Inconsistent findings, no synthesis | Meta-analysis (a moderator analysis explains the inconsistency) |
| Poor or competing theories | Test of competing predictions, or conceptual development |
| Inadequate analysis of existing data | Re-analysis with an appropriate model (e.g. multilevel, SEM, CB- vs. PLS-SEM choice) |

Close the introduction with the **current-study paragraph(s)**, 1-3 paragraphs:

- **Design:** what you do, with the key constructs defined operationally.
- **Link:** why this design answers the RQ.
- **Expectations:** what you expect and why. This can be a pointer to the hypotheses or propositions that follow.

The contributions statement (move 6 above) then follows, or is merged into this paragraph.

**Multi-study papers:** Replace the current-study paragraph with an overview of the studies. Give the number of studies, what they share, how they differ, and the sequential logic: e.g. Study 2 rules out an alternative explanation left open by Study 1, or fixes a limitation of Study 1. Each later study gets a short transitional introduction of about one paragraph in a journal article. That paragraph restates what the previous study found and what remains open, then gives the design, how it differs, and the hypotheses. Do not add a new literature review there.

**Openings:** The strategies that suit IB and marketing journals are a striking sourced fact or statistic, a puzzling contrast between cases, a historical development, or the lack of prior research. Do not open with rhetorical questions or everyday-experience anecdotes (see Section 1).

**Theses vs. articles:** In a thesis, justify decisions at greater length and give the literature review more room. In a journal article, justify the same things concisely.

### Theory / literature review
- Organise by argument, not by author. Each paragraph synthesises several sources behind one claim. Never write a chain of "Author (year) found..." sentences.
- Define key constructs inline at first use: "liability of outsidership, that is, the disadvantage firms face when...".
- Show mixed evidence explicitly, e.g. "However, their effects are not uniform. Federico et al. (2009), for example, find...". Then state what the mixed evidence implies: "This mixed evidence suggests that...".
- Move from general to specific. Summarise what is known, unknown, agreed and disputed, and prioritise seminal work: the studies that first posed the question, built the theory or developed the method.
- When one study deserves a detailed treatment (it is highly influential, or it is the closest predecessor), use this sequence: (1) why it matters, (2) its aims, method, results and conclusions, only as far as the critique needs, (3) a focused critique, (4) the implication for the present study. Critique respectfully. Every critique should lead toward the justification for the present study.
- In long reviews, add linking paragraphs. Each one summarises the section so far, ties it to the RQ, and previews the next section.
- Close the section by positioning the study: "We therefore treat disruption as a contingency that..."
- Classification decisions should be explicit and justified: "...are classified as professional network ties, because these relationships originate in..."

### Hypotheses (quantitative papers)
Pattern: theory, then mechanism, then supporting evidence, then **"Thus:"**, then the hypothesis.

- Give the mechanism in two to four sentences that each add a causal step. Cite the theory by name (e.g. stimulus-organism-response, multi-attribute utility theory, signalling theory).
- Hypothesis format: **H1.** "The positive impact of X on Y is stronger for A than for B." For split moderation: "The positive impact of some instruments (a, b) on Y is stronger for A, while that of other instruments (c) is stronger for B."
- When you do not hypothesise main effects, say why: "As these effects have received significant attention, we briefly discuss the underlying theories but do not derive hypotheses."

### Method
- Justify every design choice in the same sentence or the next: "Austria is a useful setting because..." / "...to ensure that..."
- Use a numbered rationale for sampling ("First, we focus on young startups because...").
- Report the concrete numbers: interviews, durations, rounds and dates, or effect sizes, samples and countries.
- For qualitative work, name the procedure and cite it (e.g. Saldaña's first-cycle In Vivo and second-cycle Pattern Coding; Yin's replication logic; Eisenhardt's contrasting cases). State coding rules operationally ("coded as narrow for one or two markets within a single region...").
- State what you do NOT infer: "We therefore do not attribute an entry to a particular tie merely because both were present during the same period."

### Findings (qualitative)
Use this cycle for each theme: **claim, then quote, then interpretation, then link to the literature, then (optionally) a proposition.**

- Introduce quotes with the claim they support ("...as exemplified by the following quotes:"). Attribute each one consistently, e.g. (Founder 8) or (Founder 13, interview after the pandemic). Mark omissions with [...] and insertions with [ ].
- Link to the literature with calibrated verbs:
  - *"Our findings extend this work by showing how..."*
  - *"This finding qualifies prior research that..."*
  - *"...our findings reveal an important boundary condition: ..."*
  - *"This is consistent with / in line with..."*
- Build the argument for each proposition in the paragraph before it, then introduce it with "We therefore propose:". **Proposition N:** state it in one or two sentences, including the mechanism ("because...").
- Close with a synthesis paragraph ("Taken together, the first three propositions...") that names the higher-order pattern.

### Results (quantitative)
Lead with the answer, then the statistics. Report effect sizes, CIs or SEs, and the model diagnostics reviewers expect (VIF, residual checks, robustness). Name surprises plainly ("Surprisingly, we do not find a positive effect of X on Y.") and give one plausible explanation with a citation.

### Discussion / Conclusion
1. Restate the motivation and what the study did in one or two sentences, with numbers.
2. Numbered contributions ("Our first contribution is to research on X by showing that..."), each tied to a literature and to specific findings.
3. A synthesis paragraph ("Overall, our findings show...").
4. **Implications for practice and policy.** Number them. Each is actionable and names the actor (founders, managers, policymakers). Avoid generic advice.
5. **Limitations and future research.** Start with "This study has several limitations. First, ...". Pair each limitation with a concrete, conditional research direction, e.g. "In disruptions characterized by institutional instability..., we expect...". Do not end with a vague "more research is needed".

## 4. Claim calibration

- For qualitative or cross-sectional designs, use associational language: *associated with, linked to, appears to, suggests*. Reserve *causes, drives, leads to* for designs that support them.
- Pre-empt misreadings explicitly: "This does not imply that such ties were absent...; rather, our evidence did not link them directly to..." / "We do not interpret this difference as evidence of stable founder personality traits."
- Use one hedge per claim, not stacked hedges ("may potentially suggest").
- For qualitative work, state the generalisation limits once, clearly, in Limitations. Do not scatter apologies throughout.

## 5. Citations (APA 7 unless the journal says otherwise)

- In parentheses: (Author & Author, 2020; Author et al., 2023). Order multiple citations alphabetically, then by year. Use "&" inside parentheses and "and" in the running text.
- Use "e.g.," or "see, for instance," when the list is illustrative.
- Give page numbers for every direct quote: (Galizzi et al., 2025, p. 15).
- Cite recent work (last 3-5 years) alongside classics (e.g. Granovetter, 1973; Johanson & Vahlne, 2009). Reviewers at IB and marketing journals check both.
- Never invent a reference, page number, statistic or author. If a claim needs a source you do not have, insert **[CITE: what is needed]** and flag it to the user.

## 6. Words and patterns to avoid (AI tells)

- **Vocabulary:** delve, crucial, pivotal, paramount, landscape, realm, tapestry, intricate, multifaceted, nuanced (as filler), robust (unless statistical), leverage (as a verb), underscore (overused), shed light on, pave the way, a testament to, in today's fast-paced world, it is worth noting that, notably (as a sentence opener), furthermore (repeated), in conclusion.
- **Structures:**
  - Triads of adjectives.
  - "not only X but also Y" more than once per section.
  - "This is not X; it is Y" reversals.
  - Rhetorical questions.
  - Sentences that restate the previous sentence.
  - Paragraphs ending in a one-line moral.
  - Colon-heavy headings ("Networks: A New Perspective").
- **Formatting:** no bullet points in manuscript body text. Enumerate in prose with First/Second/Third, or with (a), (b), (c) inside a sentence. No bold for emphasis.
- **Hype:** novel (used more than once), groundbreaking, significant (unless statistical), comprehensive (unless literally true).

## 7. Workflow

**When drafting:**

1. Ask for or confirm the target journal, the paper type (qualitative, survey or experiment, meta-analysis, conceptual) and the section.
2. Outline the moves from Section 3 before writing prose.
3. Draft, then run the checklist below.

**When editing Arne's text:**

- Preserve his argument, terminology and citations. Tighten; do not rewrite for its own sake.
- Fix: stacked connectors, mixed spelling, citation order, unbalanced quote marks, over-long sentences (>45 words), vague gap statements, and uncalibrated causal claims.
- Return the tracked or edited text plus a short list of substantive changes. For a .docx, use tracked changes via the docx skill.

**When responding to reviewers:**

- For each comment: thank the reviewer briefly (once, not every time), state what was changed, and give the page or section. Quote the new text where helpful.
- Be courteous but not deferential. Where you disagree, explain with evidence and citations.

## 8. Final checklist

- [ ] Each paragraph has one point, stated in its first or second sentence.
- [ ] The introduction states the Gap, Importance and Method explicitly. The gap type is named and evidenced, and the design fits the gap.
- [ ] Any "no prior research" claim is bounded and has been verified.
- [ ] Gap → RQ → contributions line up: the same concepts are named the same way in the introduction and the conclusion.
- [ ] Contributions are numbered and tied to specific literatures.
- [ ] Claims match the design (associational vs. causal).
- [ ] Spelling variant, hyphenation and capitalisation are consistent throughout.
- [ ] Citations follow APA 7 and are alphabetical within parentheses; every direct quote has a page number; there are no invented references.
- [ ] No em dashes, no words from the avoid list, no bullets in body text.
- [ ] Mean sentence length is roughly 25-28 words, with short sentences used for emphasis.
- [ ] Limitations are paired with concrete future research directions.
